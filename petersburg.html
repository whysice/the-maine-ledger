FCC Petersburg: transgender people in federal custody

Sixty-one people counted in 2023. One documented death, in 2015, after she sued over her care. Three deaths in six weeks this spring, inspectors kept out of the housing units, and a policy that has been rewritten, blocked and rewritten again.

PUBLISHED OCT 06 2026BY SAMANTHA CLAYTONREAD 16 MINSOURCES 42

In brief EDITOR'S SUMMARY

  • Sixty-one of 2,360 people at FCC Petersburg identified as transgender or intersex at the last audit, in October 2023. No newer count is public.
  • One documented death of a transgender woman, Ashley Jean Arnold, in 2015, two months after a judge ruled against her lawsuit over medical care.
  • Three deaths were announced in six weeks in May and June 2026; the bureau gives no cause and does not record gender identity in death notices.
  • A 2021 death sent two lieutenants and a nurse to prison for failing to get a man medical care over 30 hours.

Written by the editor from the records cited below, not generated by software. The full report has the sources for every line.

When outside auditors visited the federal prison complex at Petersburg, Virginia in the fall of 2023, 61 of the 2,360 people held there identified as transgender or intersex. In the three years since, the federal government has cancelled the policy that governed their care, a judge has ordered that care to continue, and the Bureau of Prisons has announced it is closing part of the complex.

What the public cannot see is how those 61 people, or whoever has replaced them, are faring. A review of Bureau of Prisons releases, court filings, inspection reports and Justice Department records found one documented death of a transgender woman at Petersburg, in 2015, after she sued over her medical care. It found no public data that would show whether there have been others. The bureau does not publish deaths by prison and gender identity, and its death notices give no cause.

The same records show three deaths at the complex in just over six weeks this spring, a 2021 death that sent two lieutenants and a nurse to prison, and inspectors who were refused entry to the housing units. Petersburg is not named in any of the national court filings on transgender care reviewed for this story.

01A complex in the middle of a shutdown

The Federal Correctional Complex at Petersburg sits on River Road in Prince George County, between Petersburg and Hopewell. It holds men in a medium security prison, a low security prison and a minimum security camp. The oldest part dates to 1932, according to the DC Corrections Information Council, which monitors District of Columbia residents held in federal prisons.

On July 1, 2026 the Bureau of Prisons announced it would close six sites, “Petersburg FCI Low” among them, “to address decades of deferred maintenance and extreme staffing challenges.” It put its maintenance backlog at more than $4 billion.

“We are a Bureau that acts,” Director William K. Marshall III said in the release. Staff at the Low “will be transferred to other units onsite or nearby.”

The release gives no timeline and does not say where the people held at the Low will go. The bureau’s population report dated Oct. 1, 2026 lists 1,629 people at the Medium and 204 at the camp. It has no line for the Low.

The union that represents federal prison workers asked Congress to stop the closures. Congress had already provided about $3 billion for personnel and $2 billion for infrastructure, American Federation of Government Employees President Everett Kelley wrote, and “those investments should be used to strengthen the federal prison system, not dismantle it.” He made the case in a July 2 letter.

02Who is held at the Medium

The Medium is one of the prisons where the bureau runs its Sex Offender Management Program. The bureau’s sex offender page lists it. When DC inspectors visited in 2014, they reported that 48 percent of the people there had been convicted of sex offenses and that it was “one of nine facilities in the country” offering the program.

The only public count of transgender people at Petersburg comes from the complex’s most recent audit under the Prison Rape Elimination Act. The auditor’s final report, submitted Dec. 18, 2023, records 2,360 people at the complex on the first day of the visit, Oct. 31, 2023. Of those, 61 identified as transgender or intersex.

For scale: the bureau told a federal court it held 2,198 transgender people across the whole system as of Feb. 20, 2025, NPR reported. It later told another judge that 1,028 people in its custody had a gender dysphoria diagnosis and that roughly 628 of them were receiving hormone therapy.

No bureau document or court filing found in this review explains why transgender people are placed at Petersburg in particular. The closest comparison is in Arizona. The Marshall Project reported in October 2024 that the federal penitentiary in Tucson, another sex offender program site, held more transgender women than any other federal prison.

The bureau council that handled questions about transgender people in custody no longer exists under its old name. A Feb. 21, 2025 bureau memo says, “The Transgender Executive Council has been renamed the Special Populations Oversight Committee (SPOC), and the associated mailbox previously utilized by the transgender population has been taken offline.”

03One death on the record

Ashley Jean Arnold died at the Medium on Feb. 24, 2015. She was 32. Prison Legal News and Vice reported the death as a suicide. No Bureau of Prisons statement on it was found.

Arnold had sued prison officials in July 2013 in federal court in Alexandria. The case is Arnold v. Wilson, No. 1:13-cv-900. According to Vice, which reviewed the court file, she asked for treatment in line with the standards of the World Professional Association for Transgender Health. She “was eventually placed on hormones in January 2014 (about six months after the suit was first filed),” Vice reported, and “the process took nearly two years.”

On Dec. 23, 2014, U.S. District Judge Leonie M. Brinkema ruled for the government. Prison Legal News reported that she found officials “were not deliberately indifferent to Arnold’s serious medical needs.” Vice quoted the ruling as finding “constitutionally adequate treatment.” Arnold appealed. She died about two months after the ruling.

A prison official is not liable “unless the official knows of and disregards an excessive risk to inmate health or safety.”
Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825 (1994), a case brought by a transgender woman in federal custody. It supplies the legal test applied in Arnold’s case.

After Arnold’s death, two people held with her who had helped with her legal work asked the Justice Department to open a criminal inquiry into what they alleged was harassment and retaliation by staff. One of them, Christopher Zoukis, wrote the Prison Legal News article. Vice wrote that the Justice Department and the Bureau of Prisons “declined to comment.” No public record of what became of the request was found.

How this compares nationally. The Justice Department’s inspector general reviewed 344 deaths in federal prisons from fiscal 2014 through 2021 and reported in February 2024 that “suicides accounted for approximately 54 percent, or 187.” More than half of those who died by suicide, 102 of 187, were housed alone. The report does not break the deaths down by prison and does not mention Petersburg.

If you or someone you know is in crisis, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

04A death that ended in convictions

One Petersburg death has been examined in open court. Nothing in the record suggests the man was transgender. The case matters here because it is the only Petersburg death found in this review in which the government has publicly established what staff did.

The man, identified by prosecutors only as W.W., was 47. He “died of injuries he sustained over the course of a 30-hour period spanning Jan. 9 and 10, 2021,” the Justice Department said in a May 2025 release.

Covington “willfully failed to ensure” that the man received medical care “even though she knew that W.W. had serious medical needs,” the department said. The outcomes for Anderson and for Covington, Farley and Blackwell come from the department’s inspector general.

Other Petersburg staff have been prosecuted for corruption. A correctional officer was sentenced in 2012 to 12 months for conspiring to distribute heroin. Two former officials were sentenced in September 2021, to four years and to 10 months, in a bribery scheme. In June 2026 federal prosecutors in Georgia announced an indictment of 12 people accused of flying contraband into 10 prisons by drone. Petersburg is on the list.

05The policy fight

Care for transgender people in federal prisons has been rewritten, blocked and rewritten again since January 2025. As of this week it is governed by a court order that the government has asked the Supreme Court to lift.

Lamberth’s first ruling did not reach the constitutional question. He found the policy likely violated the law that governs how agencies make decisions. “Neither the BOP nor the Executive Order provides any serious explanation as to why the treatment modalities covered by the Executive Order or implementing memoranda should be handled differently than any other mental health intervention,” he wrote.

Federal law limits this kind of order in a prison case to 90 days, so the plaintiffs have had to win it again and again. The current order took effect Sept. 15, 2026, which puts its expiration in mid-December unless it is renewed.

Is the order being followed? In February 2026, Uncloseted Media reported that the plaintiffs’ lawyers had “confirmed cases of noncompliance at 19 different prisons.” The article names five. Petersburg is not one of them, and the 19 are not listed.

Petersburg also does not appear in about 30 filings from the case reviewed for this story, including the complaint, the rulings and the status reports. That is an absence of evidence. It is not a finding that Petersburg is complying.

The audit gap. Federal rules require each prison to be audited under the Prison Rape Elimination Act at least once every three years. Petersburg’s last report is dated December 2023. Under the Justice Department’s December 2025 instruction, auditors have been told to hold off on ruling whether a prison meets the standards written for transgender people.

06What outside inspectors found

Petersburg has one regular outside inspector that most federal prisons lack. Because the District of Columbia has no prison of its own, its residents serve felony sentences in federal custody, and the DC Corrections Information Council visits the prisons that hold them. It has published three reports on Petersburg.

Complex, September 2022December 20211,502 people at the Medium, 770 at the Low. “The most concerning issues at FCI Petersburg are the reports of leaking roofs and serious flooding in housing units.”

The 2022 report records something else. “The CIC was not permitted to tour any general population housing units at either institution, despite regularly visiting such units at other facilities,” the inspectors wrote. Residents told them of mold and of being moved out of units at the Low because of roof damage. Staff told them the unit closures “were due to COVID.”

None of the three reports mentions transgender residents. The 2016 reports list no suicides at either prison for the period they covered.

07What the government does not count

The Bureau of Prisons announces deaths one press release at a time. The releases name the person, the sentence and the time. They do not give a cause, and they do not record gender identity. The four bureau releases linked below each describe the person as male. The February 2025 death is taken from a Progress-Index report of the bureau’s statement.

Two things stand out. Three people died in just over six weeks in May and June 2026, days before the bureau announced the Low would close. And three of the five had been at Petersburg 16 days or less.

The releases do not say how any of the five died, so nothing here establishes a cause or a link between them. The numbers do show what a reader cannot learn from the bureau’s own notices: whether a death was a suicide, an overdose, an illness or an assault, and whether the person was transgender.

The records that would answer those questions exist. The inspector general’s 2024 report describes the internal reviews the bureau conducts after a death, including a “Psychological Reconstruction” after a suicide. Those reviews are not published. The inspector general’s own dataset covers fiscal 2014 through 2021 and was released only as national totals.

No bureau release about a Petersburg death dated after June 29, 2026 was found.
